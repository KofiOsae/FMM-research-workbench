From an optical target to an arbitrary periodic cell

This guide connects a desired response to geometry, shows how to import a two-material unit cell, and builds a phase library for a flat lens. Every step states what the present solver establishes and what requires a finite-device or curved-waveguide solver.

2 · Import or draw an arbitrary periodic cell

Supported

A binary two-material pattern inside one periodic layer, extruded uniformly through that layer thickness. Import an image or 0/1 CSV, or draw with the brush. Stack up to 64 masks.

Outside this solver

A finite isolated object, sloped or curved sidewall, free-form height surface, conformal coating, or finite curved microfiber knot. These need a finite-domain or curved-waveguide solver.

  1. Add a layer and choose Imported / drawn binary mask.
  2. Choose background and feature materials. White pixels become the feature; dark pixels become the background.
  3. Import PNG/JPEG/SVG or a rectangular CSV. The image threshold creates the material mask. Inspect the reported feature fill.
  4. Check periodic continuity: the left edge touches the next cell’s right edge, and the top touches the neighboring bottom.
  5. Set physical periods and thickness. Pixel count is the mask representation; period converts it to micrometres.
  6. Raise geometry grid and Fourier budget separately. Sharp corners and one-pixel gaps can require many harmonics.
  7. Save the project. The exact binary mask is embedded in its JSON.
Geometry gate: inspect the mask canvas and material contours on field maps. The 3D overview is schematic for imported masks.

3 · Flat-lens design with a phase library

The solver calculates an infinite periodic cell and its complex diffraction amplitudes. The assistant uses the common local periodic approximation (LPA): each candidate is solved as though all neighbors were identical, then assigned across the lens.

Ideal focusing phase
φ(r) = −(2π/λ)[√(f²+r²)−f] mod 2π
NA = noutR/√(R²+f²)
  1. Create a subwavelength cell with only the intended transmitted order propagating. Use normal incidence and coherent s or p polarization.
  2. Open Sweeps & design → Phase library and flat-lens assignment. Choose pillar width, radius, or thickness.
  3. Use 20–50 cells initially and enforce minimum specular power, such as T₀ ≥ 0.7.
  4. Inspect phase versus geometry, largest uncovered phase gap, and discontinuities near resonances.
  5. Enter radius and focal length. The assistant optimizes a physically irrelevant global phase offset and assigns the nearest usable cell.
  6. Export JSON and radial CSV. RMS phase error estimates quantization; mean selected T₀ is an efficiency proxy, not focused power.
  7. Repeat at larger Fourier budget and finer grid, then over wavelength and polarization when required.
  8. Validate the assembled device. High NA, abrupt neighbors, strong coupling, and small lenses require supercell or finite full-wave validation plus near-to-far propagation.
Example acceptance plan: phase gap < 30°, RMS assignment error < 10°, minimum T₀ > 0.7, feature size above fabrication minimum, and stability under expected wavelength, angle, and dimensional error. Choose thresholds for the application.

4 · A systematic geometry-thinking loop

  1. Write the observable: average A from 8–12 µm, T₀ phase at 1.55 µm, or Q under ±1° spread.
  2. Choose the mechanism: impedance match, interference, guided resonance, localized resonance, Bragg gap, quasi-BIC, or propagation phase.
  3. Select degrees of freedom: period controls momentum/diffraction; thickness controls propagation/confinement; shape controls symmetry/polarization/coupling; n and k control storage/loss.
  4. Use the simplest control: uniform TMM, analytic slab mode, or Fresnel limit.
  5. Explore coarsely, then refine: never optimize one failed or grazing-cutoff point.
  6. Validate the claim: numerical convergence, material uncertainty, fabrication tolerance, and solver scope.

5 · The attached ultra-high-Q microfiber knot

The attachment shows a finite, curved, self-coupled microfiber resonator with ring diameter DR, fiber diameter DF, coupling length Lk, and sub-knot lengths L₁ and L₂. Periodic layered RCWA cannot calculate its bend radiation, contact coupling, mechanical deformation, or polarization mixing.

The Ring and microfiber-knot spectral metrics calculator can analyze measured or externally simulated spectra:

Qloaded = λ₀/Δλ
FSR ≈ λ₀²/(ngL)
Finesse = FSR/Δλ
Photon lifetime τ = Q/ω₀
  1. Fit a resolved resonance and enter center and linewidth in the same wavelength units.
  2. Enter group index and the actual knot round-trip optical path.
  3. Compare predicted and measured FSR. A mismatch can expose the wrong path, group index, or sub-loop family.
  4. Optional transmission depth returns both under-coupled and over-coupled Q branches because intensity alone cannot distinguish them.
  5. For geometry prediction, combine a full-vector microfiber mode, bent-waveguide radiation model, contact-region directional-coupler supermodes, and a multi-loop transfer matrix for L₁ and L₂.

The 2026 paper reports Q up to 3.9×10⁷ and discusses sensing, nonlinear optics, quantum devices, and low-threshold lasers. Reproduction also depends on fabrication and environmental control.
