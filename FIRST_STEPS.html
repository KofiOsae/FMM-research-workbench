Start with a physical question

The workspaces solve different physical problems. Choose the one that matches your question before entering a geometry. Use Run from here in the persistent toolbar to start a calculation without scrolling to its card. Jump to operation opens its settings; Focus selected hides other cards when you need a quieter view.

All lengths below are in micrometres: 0.375 µm = 375 nm. A value of 0.375 nm is a thousand times smaller. Phase is defined for complex amplitudes; an intensity or incoherent polarization average has no single optical phase.

Three independent resolutions: wavelength/angle points resolve a plotted feature; Fourier harmonics resolve the electromagnetic expansion; geometry cells resolve material boundaries. Increasing one does not substitute for the others.
Lesson 1 · analytic teaching control

A thin film you can verify by hand

Question: can you predict where a lossless film reflects most strongly, and then recover that behavior numerically?

SettingValue
Top / bottom mediumn = 1 / n = 1.5
One finite layerUniform, Constant n = 2, thickness 0.125 µm
IncidenceNormal, s polarization
Spectrum0.80–1.20 µm, 101 wavelengths
  1. In Scattering & fields, select the uniform preset. Set the layer and surrounding indices above. Confirm that the preview contains one film.
  2. Enter the spectrum limits in Settings. Choose Transfer-matrix spectrum from the persistent run selector.
  3. Inspect R and T near 1.00 µm. Record R + T + A and the wavelength of maximum reflection.
  4. Change thickness to 0.375 µm and rerun. This is another odd-quarter-wave thickness at the same target wavelength.
  5. Open Sweeps & design, vary layer thickness at fixed wavelength 1.00 µm, then add wavelength as the second axis. Look for diagonal interference bands.
Expected: at λ = 1 µm and d = 0.125 or 0.375 µm, R ≈ 0.206612, T ≈ 0.793388, A ≈ 0. These numbers follow from the lossless transfer matrix. An air/glass interface alone has R = 0.04.

The round-trip film phase is 4πnd/λ. Increasing d shifts an interference feature to longer wavelength. The condition d = λ/(4n) gives the first odd-quarter-wave thickness here. A thicker film can give the same reflection at one wavelength while having a different spectrum.

Design decision: select a thickness using the entire desired wavelength band. A single target value cannot uniquely determine thickness.

Lesson 2 · published numerical benchmark

Reproduce the square lattice of dielectric rods

This is the square-rod example from the MIT Photonic Bands tutorial, associated with Chapter 5 of Photonic Crystals: Molding the Flow of Light. The reference uses radius/a = 0.2 and ε = 12 in air. Its reported first TM gap spans a/λ ≈ 0.28262–0.41933. Its first TE frequency at reciprocal coordinates (0.3, 0.3) is 0.372604. Those are external comparison values, not promises of exact agreement on a coarse workbench grid.

  1. Open Band structure, load the named example, and check every value above.
  2. Run the band calculation. Locate the orange TM branches and look between the first two bands.
  3. Increase the Fourier order at fixed geometry grid. Compare the upper edge of band 1 and lower edge of band 2. Then refine the geometry grid separately.
  4. Open Bloch band eigenmode field. Select a polarization, band, and k point. Compare fields on either side of a candidate gap.
  5. Save the raw bands, settings, and your convergence comparison. The supplied benchmark_mpb.py checks the quoted TE frequency independently of the plotted path.
Expected pattern: a substantial separation between the first two TM bands. TE and TM use the app's 2D convention: TE has Hz, TM has Ez. A TM gap alone does not exclude TE propagation.

Design exercise: choose a target wavelength of 1.55 µm at normalized frequency 0.35. The scale relation a = (a/λ)λ gives a = 0.5425 µm and r = 0.1085 µm. Keep the dimensionless geometry fixed. A real material's dispersion requires reevaluating its index at the target wavelength.

The model extends indefinitely along the rod axis. A fabricated finite-height pillar array needs a finite-stack calculation and can radiate vertically. Check wavevectors inside the Brillouin zone before claiming a complete gap.
Lesson 3 · interpretation

What should you look for in a band diagram?

FeatureInterpretationDesign relevance / check
GapNo band of the chosen polarization in an interval over the tested wavevectorsCandidate reflection or confinement window. Check all directions and polarizations required by the device.
Flat segmentSmall frequency change with k; small group-velocity component along that directionPotential slow propagation and stronger interaction per length. Assess loss, bandwidth, and disorder sensitivity separately.
Steep segmentLarger directional frequency derivativeUseful for transport. Compare derivatives with actual k distances, especially at changes of path direction.
CrossingTwo frequencies coincide or nearly coincideInspect symmetry and field patterns. Sorting eigenvalues by frequency alone can exchange mode identities.
Avoided crossingBranches approach and separateInspect how fields exchange character. A picture alone does not establish a quantitative coupling rate.
Bright field regionLarge value of the selected normalized field quantityCompare overlap with the intended active material. H-field intensity does not directly equal electric energy or emitter coupling.

In a periodic unit cell, the left and right boundaries are neighboring copies. Four bright corners can be one repeated pattern, rather than four independent localized cavities. Eigenmode phase has an arbitrary global offset; relative phase and nodal structure carry the spatial information.

Try: change radius/a from 0.20 to 0.18, then 0.22. Record the same two band edges after convergence. Which change moves the gap center toward your target? Which widens the useful interval?

Lesson 4 · method-based teaching geometry

A guided mode must survive numerical refinement

The rectangular solver uses the vector finite-difference method of Fallahkhair, Li, and Murphy (2008). The following lossless silicon-like geometry is our training exercise; it is not a reproduction of a figure in that paper.

InputValue
Wavelength1.55 µm
Core width × height0.55 × 0.22 µm
Core / substrate / upper-cladding index3.47 / 1.44 / 1
Left-right / top / bottom padding1.10 / 1.10 / 1.10 µm
Mesh / eigenpairs / boundary0.020 µm / 2 / zero-field boundary
  1. In Guided modes, choose the rectangular vector solver, enter these values, and run.
  2. Record the actual cell count and spacing together with neff, TE-like fraction, and core electric fraction.
  3. Keep padding fixed; decrease mesh spacing. Compare the same spatial mode, rather than assuming mode number alone identifies it.
  4. Restore the comparison mesh and increase all pads to 1.40 µm. This isolates sensitivity to the outer boundary.
  5. Inspect field components and relative phases. A mode that moves strongly with padding needs further investigation.
Training observation, not a converged reference: at a reported 0.020 µm mesh our session produced neff ≈ 2.4753 for the first mode, about 89% TE-like and 56.5% core electric fraction. Use these only to identify the intended example.

TE-like quantifies field orientation in a hybrid vector mode. Core electric fraction measures localization of |E|²; it is not a collected-power fraction. An imaginary effective index around 10−16 in this lossless calculation is numerical roundoff. Identical outputs after an input change can be physically stable, but first compare the returned mesh and geometry to establish that the new settings were applied.

Lesson 5 · exploratory teaching design

From a slab mode to a guided resonance

A plane wave at normal incidence has no in-plane momentum. Can a grating provide the momentum needed to excite a guided field?

Physical stack, top to bottomSetting
Top mediumAir, n = 1
Layer 1Stripe, 0.060 µm thick, air background, silicon nitride feature, width fraction 0.50
Layer 2Uniform silicon nitride, 0.140 µm thick
Bottom medium / x periodn = 1.45 / 0.503 µm
Initial illumination / spectrums, normal incidence / 0.78–0.88 µm, 101 points
  1. Build this stack and confirm that the stripe's background differs from its feature. A stripe with SiN in both entries has no index grating.
  2. Run a coarse spectrum. Locate a reflection feature and check its Fourier and geometry convergence.
  3. Run an angle–wavelength map over −12° to +12°. Begin with 21 × 41 points, then increase resolution in a smaller region of interest.
  4. Compare vertical fields at the feature and away from it. Keep normalization conventions visible.
  5. Use the adaptive or joint fit only when the window contains the feature's flanks and background. Expand the window if the center or linewidth reaches its bounds.

The first-order estimate β ≈ 2π/Λ gives Λ ≈ λ/neff. With the earlier slab estimate neff = 1.689343 at λ = 0.85 µm, this suggests Λ ≈ 0.503 µm. Patterning and the full vertical stack shift the actual resonance, so use this as a starting estimate.

A centered stripe of width 0.50 spans x/Λ = 0.25–0.75. Those are material edges; dark field bands need not sit at the same positions. Opposite in-plane propagation directions can produce two angle-dependent branches. Their convergence at normal incidence does not by itself prove a BIC.

Q gate: Q = λ0/Δλ is meaningful only for a stable, resolved fit with a documented linewidth definition. A tiny residual over a small part of a broad peak can give a misleading Q. Do not reuse the inconsistent Q values observed during the training session.
Lesson 6 · synthetic inverse problem

Recover thickness, then ask what is identifiable

  1. Use the lossless uniform film from Lesson 1, set thickness to 0.260 µm, and export 31 wavelengths from 0.80 to 1.20 µm with R and T.
  2. Import that spectrum into Measurements & fit. Change the model thickness to 0.200 µm and fit thickness over an interval containing 0.260 µm.
  3. Inspect the overlay and residuals. Record the recovered thickness, then release both thickness and refractive index in the multi-parameter fit.
  4. Read the parameter correlation table before interpreting uncertainty. For Bayesian runs, inspect R-hat, effective sample size, acceptance, and chain behavior together.
Expected: the same-model noiseless test should recover 0.260 µm with a very small residual. This verifies the fitting workflow; it does not reproduce experimental uncertainty.

Thickness and refractive index both affect optical thickness nd. They can compensate for each other, producing a strong negative correlation. Add independent index data, a wider spectral interval, or additional angles/polarizations to improve discrimination. A nearly perfect fit does not establish that each parameter is tightly constrained.

Tolerance exercise: vary thickness around 0.125 µm with standard deviation 0.005 µm. Track reflection-peak wavelength and peak height separately. Peak position can shift substantially while the peak height stays similar. Performance at each sample's own peak differs from performance at one fixed operating wavelength.

Twenty bootstrap or fabrication samples are useful for a demonstration, but tail percentiles can be unstable. Repeat with more samples and seeds when making a quantitative uncertainty claim. Bayesian intervals depend on the stated priors, bounds, and likelihood.
Lesson 7 · conditional estimate and planar calculation

Faster emission does not guarantee brighter collection

  1. In Cavity estimate, enter λc = λem = 1.55 µm, n = 3.4, Q = 1000, V = 0.1 µm³, overlap = 1.
  2. Record the ideal resonant Purcell estimate, then change only emitter wavelength to 1.56 µm.
  3. For the separate planar LDOS solver, load the gold SPR uniform stack. Fix distance, orientation, wavelength, and collection NA before comparing results.
  4. Change perpendicular to parallel dipole orientation. Compare total normalized decay and collection fraction together. Export inputs with every comparison.
Formula check: the Q/V example gives FP ≈ 71.998 on resonance; our detuned training case gave a single-mode contribution ≈ 0.435. These values are conditional on the supplied Q, V, and overlap.

The planar calculation includes propagating and evanescent contributions to decay. A metal can increase total decay through channels that an objective does not collect. Use (Γ/Γ0)η to compare normalized collected decay under the same emitter assumptions. This quantity alone does not predict brightness under arbitrary pumping or intrinsic nonradiative loss.

A negative correction density in part of a wavevector plot represents interference relative to the reference medium; it is not a negative total decay rate. Inspect refinement and the integration range when interpreting a narrow evanescent peak.

Lesson 8 · coupled features

Track several resonances without swapping their identities

  1. Open Sweeps & design, choose the physical parameter, and start with three parameter values over a narrow interval.
  2. Open Track several resonance branches. Retain only as many components as the global scan visibly supports.
  3. Inspect BIC preference, component uncertainty, and the normalized coarse-field overlap used by the assignment.
  4. Increase the parameter and wavelength sampling separately. A branch that disappears, jumps by several linewidths, or changes field shape is not securely identified.
  5. For an apparent avoided crossing, report the minimum separation together with both linewidths and the sampling uncertainty.
The assignment uses fitted centers, linewidths, and driven intensity overlap. It is a practical tracking diagnostic, not a complex pole or normalized quasinormal-mode overlap.
Lesson 9 · statistical model checking

A posterior must predict the observations

  1. Use repeated measurements to estimate σ and inspect residual correlation versus wavelength.
  2. Run independent chains with documented priors and bounds. Use AR(1) correlation only when repeat-measurement residuals support it.
  3. Require rank-normalized split R̂ near one and inspect both bulk and tail effective sample sizes.
  4. Compare measured points with the exported 95% posterior predictive intervals. Coverage that is very low signals a poor likelihood, optical model, material model, or calibration.
  5. Repeat with another seed and a plausible alternative noise model before quoting a narrow credible interval.

A posterior interval describes uncertainty conditional on the entered model. It does not include an omitted layer, incorrect dispersion, angular spread, polarization impurity, or a wrong likelihood.

Lesson 10 · fabrication-aware design

Optimize a distribution rather than one perfect geometry

  1. Choose bounded variables and optical objectives. First run one fabrication sample per candidate to locate a useful region.
  2. Enter one-standard-deviation fabrication uncertainty beside each variable and raise samples per candidate.
  3. The robust loss combines the sample mean with a user-selected variability penalty. Inspect mean, standard deviation, and worst sampled loss.
  4. Rerun with another seed and more samples. Then use the separate tolerance workspace on the selected geometry.
  5. Validate the final design at reserved wavelengths and with tighter Fourier/grid settings.
Design rule: prefer a slightly lower nominal peak when it materially reduces sensitivity and improves the worst credible fabricated device.
